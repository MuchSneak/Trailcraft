Working the trail

Before you climb down

  • Mark the exact spot the deer was standing and the direction it ran, from the stand, before your memory rewrites it.
  • Note the last place you saw it and listen. A crash inside 15 seconds usually means lungs or heart.
  • Watch the tail. Clamped down and running low often means a good hit; flagging high often means a poor one.

At the hit site

  • Find the arrow before anything else. Blood, bubbles, green matter and smell on the shaft tell you more than the first fifty yards of trail.
  • Hair color reads like a map — coarse dark brown from the back, long grey-brown from the ribs, white and short from the brisket or belly.
  • Flag first blood and every drop after. If you lose the trail, the flags give you a direction of travel to grid off of.

When it goes cold

  • Go back to last blood and grid in arcs, not straight lines. Check both sides of any creek, road or fence.
  • Wounded deer bed facing their backtrail and head for water and thick cover, usually downhill.
  • Circling birds the next morning are worth a drive. So is a tracking dog — check whether yours is a state that allows leashed blood-tracking dogs before you need one.
